## The problem Templates solve

Some work is different every time. Most work isn't.

Every time your organization onboards a customer, launches a product, runs a
security review, or ships a feature, somebody recreates roughly the same set of
workstreams and items by hand. The shape is identical; only a handful of details
change — the customer's name, the target date, which team is doing it, who owns
it.

A **Template** captures that shape once. When you need it again, you pick the
template, answer a short form, and DevStride creates the whole tree — correctly
typed, correctly dated, correctly assigned — under wherever you point it.

::alert{type="tip" title="The one-sentence version"}
A template is a **saved tree of workstreams and work items** whose fields are
either **fixed** (the same every time) or **variable** (asked for when the
template is applied).
::

## The three ideas

Almost everything in Templates follows from three ideas. Learn these and the
rest of this guide is detail.

### 1. A template is a tree, not a document

A template holds real DevStride nodes — workstreams, work items of a real work
type (Epic, Feature, Story, …), and their sub-items. Each node carries the same
fields a real item carries: title, description, dates, team, assignee, board,
priority, tags, estimates, point effort and custom fields.

When you apply the template, each node becomes a real item. Nothing is
"linked" back to the template afterwards — applying a template is a **one-time
instantiation**, like a rubber stamp. Editing the template later does not touch
work you already created from it.

### 2. A template references your existing configuration

A template does not invent its own work types, priorities, tags, teams or custom
fields. It **points at the ones your organization already has**. That is what
makes an applied template look exactly like hand-built work: it *is* your
configuration.

The trade-off is that a template can go stale — if somebody archives the team a
template pins, the template needs attention. DevStride tracks that for you; see
[Keeping templates healthy](/workspace-administration/templates/applying-a-template#when-a-template-cannot-be-applied).

### 3. Every field is either Fixed or Variable

This is the heart of the feature.

* **Fixed** — the value is baked into the template. "Every onboarding has a
  *Run the kickoff call* sub-item" is fixed. You never type it again.
* **Variable** — the template *asks* for the value when it is applied.
  "Which customer? Which go-live date? Which team?" are variables.

Getting this split right is what turns a template from a slightly faster
copy-paste into something genuinely reusable. [Variables](/workspace-administration/templates/variables)
is the guide to that.

## Finding Templates

Templates live on their own page. Open it from the left sidebar under **Admin**
by clicking **Templates**.

![The Templates page listing four templates with their audience, owner, rooting and variable count](/images/templates/templates-list.png)

Each row tells you, at a glance, everything you need in order to decide whether
a template is the one you want:

| Column | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Template** | The name, description, and a health glyph — a green tick for **Healthy**, an amber warning for **Needs attention**. |
| **Audience** | Who can see it: **Private**, **Shared**, or **Organization**. See [Sharing](/workspace-administration/templates/sharing-templates). |
| **Owner** | Who authored it. Your own templates read **You**. |
| **Starts with** | The node type at the top of the template — a Workstream, or a work item of a specific work type. |
| **Applies to** | Where the template is allowed to be placed. A Workstream-rooted template can go under any workstream; a Feature-rooted one can only go under a workstream or an Epic. |
| **Variables** | How many values the template will ask you for. |
| **Actions** | Share, Duplicate, Edit and Delete. Any action you are not allowed to take is shown disabled with a tooltip naming the reason — never as a button that fails when you press it. |

The tabs above the table filter the list:

* **All** — everything you can see.
* **Mine** — templates you authored.
* **Shared with me** — templates someone shared with you or your team specifically.
* **Organization** — templates shared with the whole organization.

## Who can do what

Templates use two independent layers of access. Both must allow an action
before you can take it.

**1. Permissions (what your role lets you do at all)**

| Permission | Grants |
| --- | --- |
| **Templates** *(module access)* | See the Templates entry in the sidebar and open the page. |
| **Create new templates** | Create a template, and duplicate an existing one. |
| **Update and delete templates you authored** | Edit and delete your own templates. |
| **Update and delete other members' templates** | Admin override — edit and delete anyone's template. |

By default, both **Admin** and **Member** roles can access Templates and manage
their own; Admins additionally get the override.

**2. Sharing (who a given template is visible to)**

Independently of your role, each template carries its own access list — private
to its author by default, and shareable with named people, whole teams, or the
entire organization at either **Can use** or **Can edit** level. A template you
have not been given access to simply does not appear for you.

::alert{type="note" title="Applying is gated by the target, not the template"}
Being able to *see* a template is not enough to apply it. You also need
permission to create workstreams and items **at the place you are applying it
to**. That is deliberate: a template must never become a way around item
permissions.
::
