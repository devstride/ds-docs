## 📧 Forward it in, and the ticket belongs to the customer

Your customers email people, not addresses. They reply to the founder who onboarded them; they
write to the CSR they trust rather than to your support address. That work is real, but the desk
cannot see it — nothing to assign, nothing on a clock, nothing a colleague can pick up while the
recipient is on holiday.

Forwarding it in used to make the record worse rather than better. The ticket opened in the name
of the **colleague who forwarded it**: their address as the requester, their covering note
published as the customer's own words, and the "we've received your request" email going back to
your own employee. The person actually waiting for an answer appeared nowhere on it.

That is fixed. When someone on your team forwards a customer's email to a **channel address**,
DevStride reads the original message out of the forward and opens the ticket in the **customer's**
name. It takes the subject the customer wrote, not `Fwd: …`. The acknowledgement goes to the
customer's own address, so their reply threads straight back onto the ticket. Their **requester**
record is created or matched as usual and linked to their **company** by email domain. From there
it is an ordinary ticket — Inbox, routing, SLA, automations, the portal — and your colleague stays
on it as a **watcher**, with whatever they typed above the forward kept internally, never shown to
the customer.

It works mid-conversation too. Forward a customer's reply into an existing ticket and it posts as
the customer's own comment, stamping the customer-reply watermark, re-arming the next-response
target and firing your reply automations exactly as a direct reply would. Forward it as a new
message to the channel address to get that: replying to a DevStride internal-note notification
stays an internal note.

This is on for every organization with a Service Desk email address. There is no setting to find
and nothing to switch on.

**Where DevStride will not guess.** Crediting the wrong person is worse than crediting nobody, so
attribution is deliberately narrow. A forward is credited only when it comes from an active member
of your organization and the message passes DMARC — if your own mail domain publishes no DMARC
record, forwards from it are never credited. A forward carrying two original messages credits
neither. And the customer's words are *published* only when the original travels as an attached
message; an ordinary inline forward still opens the ticket in the customer's name and still sends
their acknowledgement, but keeps the text internal until someone publishes it. In every one of
those cases the ticket is still created, owned by the colleague who forwarded it, with its text
kept internal. Nothing is dropped, and nobody is emailed on a guess.

## 🎫 Turn any item into a Service Desk ticket

Support work does not only arrive by email. A bug gets raised in Slack and filed as a story, and a
week later you learn a named customer is blocked on it. Until now the only way to get that onto the
desk was to raise a parallel ticket and keep two records in step by hand.

**Convert to ticket** attaches a customer to an item you already have — and attaching the customer
*is* the conversion. The item keeps its number, title, work type, board, status, comments, history
and links; what switches on is everything keyed to having a customer. It appears in the Inbox, the
customer sees it in the portal, the Description tab gains its Internal and Customer split, and the
composer gains **Reply to customer**.

The dialog asks for the customer — pick an existing requester, or type a name and email to create
one — an optional channel address so replies carry the right branding, and a customer-visible
description pre-filled with a copy of the internal one, so the common case is an edit rather than a
retype. Converting sends the customer nothing at that moment.

The action sits on the item right-click menu, in the item drawer, and in the bulk-edit menu, which
converts up to 25 at a time and is deliberately hidden under **Select all**. It needs **Manage
Requesters** and appears once Service Desk is enabled for your organization. One thing to know
before you click: it cannot be undone.

## 👁️ Publish your internal description to the customer

Keeping two descriptions in step by hand is the kind of chore people stop doing, and a stale
customer description is worse than none. A new switch at the top of an item's Description tab —
**Show internal description to the customer** — copies the internal text into the customer-visible
one on every save, sanitized on the way out, and makes the customer copy read-only so the two
cannot quietly drift apart.

It is off by default on every item, new and existing. Turning it on requires **Send Public Replies**
or **Manage Requesters**, because it publishes internal content; turning it off needs only ordinary
edit rights, so anyone who spots a problem can stop it immediately. Turning it off deliberately
keeps the last published copy rather than retracting it — if something should never have been
published, clear the customer description as well.

The switch is not limited to tickets. It appears on any work item, which is what makes a non-ticket
item on a published list worth opening.

## 📋 Published lists are a real table now

A list you publish to a customer's portal renders as a table with a sticky header instead of a
column of titles: number, title, status, created, updated and channel, drawn from the saved view you
published and filtered to those six customer-safe fields. Anything else your view carried — boards,
assignee, estimates, custom fields — is dropped before the list leaves DevStride.

Every row opens a detail panel with the item's status, dates and customer-visible description, and
that panel has its own address, so a customer can bookmark it or paste it to a colleague and land
straight on it. Where they are allowed to converse on the request, the panel offers **Open
conversation**.

**Row order no longer carries your internal ranking.** Published lists have always hidden the
customer rank and importance fields, because they show how your desk prioritizes one account against
another — but the query behind the list still *ordered* rows by that hidden rank. The ranking you
had chosen not to show was fully readable as position. Rows now order by last updated, or by your
saved view's own sort when it is by created, updated or title, and nothing about how you rank work
reaches a portal.

Visibility is still per company: a customer whose company holds no grant on a list sees no such
section at all. Nothing new to enable beyond Service Desk.

## 🔒 Files on a public request form are scanned before anyone can open them

A file attached to a public, link-only request form used to be signed straight into your
organization's public-read media bucket: roughly a gigabyte per file, no malware scan, readable by
anyone holding the URL the moment it landed, and the only content control a blocklist that a
`; charset=` suffix walked straight past.

Anonymous uploads now land in a private quarantine bucket, are malware-scanned, and reach the ticket
only on a clean verdict. The trade is a set of real limits your submitters will notice: **25 MB per
file**, an allow-list of 24 file types — SVG, video and anything unlisted are refused before the
upload starts — at most **20 files** per submission, and a short wait while each file is scanned. An
upload whose scan verdict never arrives now reports a definite error instead of sitting at
"scanning".

This applies to any organization publishing a public request form; it does not depend on the Service
Desk switch.

## 🎟️ Request forms say what they do

The form option once called **Capture requester** is now **Create as Service Desk ticket**, and its
help text leads with the outcome: each submission becomes a ticket from the person who submitted it,
the form gains required name and email fields, and the ticket comes under your SLA policies. Forms
configured that way now carry a **Ticket** badge in Settings → Request Forms.

Two fixes alongside it. A private ticket-intake form can now actually be submitted by staff on a
customer's behalf — the internal picker renders the requester name and email it has always required
but never showed, so Submit is no longer greyed out with nothing to explain it. And the message a
public submitter sees on success now matches the email that was actually sent them.

## 🛡️ A colleague's address cannot be erased as though it were a customer

Now that a member of your own team can own a requester record, erasing one needs a guard.
**Anonymize** and **Merge** now refuse outright when a requester's email address belongs to an active
member of your organization, and say why: erasure is keyed by address, and that address also appears
on records belonging to your team and on other customers' tickets. No role lifts the refusal. If you
need such an identity erased, contact DevStride support so it can be done without touching the shared
records.

## 🐛 Also fixed

- New GitHub branch events with long or accented branch names no longer stop linking to their item
- A multi-file upload no longer discards the files that succeeded when one of them fails, or leaves the field spinning
- Bulk and background item updates can no longer roll back a ticket's requester, SLA clock or verification state
- Description attachments no longer fail to register when an item's asset-group name or position is already taken
- A hard-bounced internal notification no longer switches off a colleague's ticket email
